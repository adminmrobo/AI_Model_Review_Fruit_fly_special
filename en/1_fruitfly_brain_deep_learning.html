Deep Learning A to Z · Fruit Fly Special 1

How does a fruit fly smell, see, and recognize?

Even inside a fruit fly's head, no bigger than a speck, signals travel along set paths. The antennae and compound eyes turn the world into electrical signals, the nerves sort and carry those signals, and the brain recognizes "that's food" or "that's danger." And the rules behind those judgments weren't handed down by anyone — they were learned from experience. Follow this path, and you'll naturally see how artificial neural networks grow from an ultra-simple model into multilayer deep learning.

Why learn neural networks from a fruit fly?

The human brain is too big, and computer neural networks are too abstract. The fruit fly is just the right size in between. You can take in the whole path from sense organs to behavior at a glance, and you can actually switch individual neurons along that path on and off in experiments.

Reason 1Its full wiring diagram is public

In 2024, a wiring diagram (connectome) of every neuron and connection in the fruit fly brain was completed. In neural-network terms, it's like being able to see a model's entire architecture and connections.

Reason 2Input to output is short and clear

Smelling something and approaching it, seeing a looming shadow and taking off. The path of senses → nerves → brain → behavior is short, so it's easy to follow step by step.

Reason 3You can switch specific neurons on and off

Genetic tools can stop specific neurons from working. It's like deleting one unit from a neural network and seeing how its performance changes.

Why does every sense become an electrical signal?

Light is photons, smell is molecules, sound is vibrations in the air, and taste is substances dissolved in water. They're all different kinds of stimuli, yet by the time they reach the brain they all have the same form: changes in the voltage across a neuron's membrane, and electrical pulses (spikes).

Why electricity? Five reasons

  1. A common language: light, smell, sound and taste all have to be converted into the same form so the brain can mix and compare them in one place. It's the same reason deep learning turns photos, text and sound into lists of numbers.
  2. It's fast: at the speed odor molecules drift and spread through the body, you could never dodge a fly swatter. Electrical signals carried by flows of ions travel from the tip of the body to the brain in an instant.
  3. It doesn't fade over distance: a spike is regenerated again and again as it travels along the axon, so its size doesn't shrink even over a long path.
  4. Strength is expressed as "how many times": every spike is the same size; a stronger stimulus just makes the neuron fire more often. Conveying strength by count rather than size makes it robust to noise. This firing rate corresponds to an artificial neuron's "output number."
  5. Strength can be adjusted at the synapse: in the gap between two neurons, electricity is converted into chemicals (neurotransmitters) that cross over. How much gets transmitted, and how effectively, is exactly the weight — and learning means changing this transmission efficiency.

There are exceptions. Neurons that send signals only a short distance, like the photoreceptors in the compound eye, transmit by raising and lowering their voltage continuously instead of spiking. Pick "Light" to see the difference.

Are the rules given, or are they learned?

There are two ways to get a computer to do a job. A person can write out the rules one by one (rule-based), or you can give it only examples and outcomes and let it find the rules on its own (learning-based). Deep learning is the second way, and the fruit fly brain contains both.

Rule-based (Rule base)

Someone writes the rules in advance

If fruit scent > 0.5 → approach
If carbon dioxide > 0.3 → avoid
Otherwise → stay still

The rules are visible, so they're easy to understand. But you have to know every possible situation in advance, and when the environment changes, whoever wrote the rules has to fix them by hand. In a situation the rules don't cover, it's helpless.

In the fruit fly: the innate responses of the lateral horn. Evolution wrote these rules, and a single fly can never change them in its lifetime.

Learning-based (Learning)

It looks at outcomes and fixes its own rules

1. Predict with the current values and act
2. See the actual outcome (food +1, electric shock −1)
3. value ← value + η × (outcome − prediction)

No rules are written directly. Instead, the weights are adjusted by "how wrong the prediction was." Repeat this, and the rules appear inside the weights on their own. If the environment changes, it simply learns again.

In the fruit fly: the mushroom body. When punishment or reward arrives, dopamine neurons send a signal that changes the Kenyon cell → output neuron connections. Whether this signal actually computes a prediction error like "outcome − prediction" is still being researched; here we simplify it to a prediction-error rule to make it easier to understand.

Where are learned rules written?

The right-hand fly's "rules" aren't written down as sentences anywhere. All there is are two approach values — that is, two weights. Deep learning models are exactly the same. Millions or billions of weights are the rules, so they perform well, but it's hard to pull out "why it decided that" in human words. Rule-based systems are easy to explain; learning-based systems are easy to adapt. The fruit fly uses both: it gets by on innate rules in unfamiliar situations, and corrects them with learned rules as experience builds up.

The model ladder — climb four rungs in order

With each rung, one more ability seen in the fruit fly is added. On each rung you'll operate the model yourself, run into a problem it can't solve, and then move up to the next rung.

1Ultra-simple

One input, one connection — smell carbon dioxide, avoid it

Model: y = w × xFruit fly: CO₂ avoidance

In the fruit fly

The fruit fly's antennae have dedicated sensory neurons that detect only carbon dioxide. Their signals converge on a single glomerulus in the antennal lobe, and the fly avoids carbon dioxide without having to learn it. Here we boil this path down to "one sensor → one connection → behavior."

In a neural network

This is the smallest possible model: it outputs 1 if the input x multiplied by the weight w exceeds the threshold θ, and 0 otherwise. The weight means "how loudly should I listen to this input," and the threshold means "how easily should I react."

CO₂ concentration x
Connection strength w
Threshold θ

Explained for beginners

In this model, "learning" means setting w and θ to the right values. The fruit fly is born with these values already tuned by evolution. Make w large and it flees even faint carbon dioxide; make it small and the CO₂ has to be strong before it flees.

Its limits are clear, too. This model looks at exactly one input. It can't solve the problems a fruit fly actually faces — ones that require weighing several signals together, like "There's a fruit scent and a vinegar scent at the same time. Is this worth eating?" So we add more inputs.

2Simple

One neuron weighing several inputs — the perceptron

Model: y = σ(w₁x₁ + w₂x₂ + b)Fruit fly: judging food odors

In the fruit fly

Ripe fruit gives off both a fruity scent (esters) and a vinegar scent (acetic acid). Suppose one neuron receives signals from two glomeruli and judges "is this the smell of food?" It gives the two signals different weights, adds them up, and then decides.

In a neural network

Multiply each of several inputs by its weight, add them up, add a bias b, then use the sigmoid σ to turn the result into a probability between 0 and 1. The only boundary this model can draw is a single straight line on the plane at right.

What does "learning" actually do? Four steps

  1. Predict: compute the score for one odor with the current w₁, w₂, b.
  2. Error: measure the difference between the correct answer (food 1, not food 0) and the predicted probability.
  3. Assign blame: multiply the error by each input's size to find how much each weight contributed to the mistake.
  4. Fix: nudge each weight a little (by the learning rate η) in the opposite direction. Repeat this for every odor.

Pick the "Hard" problem and train. Real fruit flies approach faint vinegar odors but avoid ones that are too strong. That makes the food region a band between two lines, and a single line can't cut out a band. No matter how long you train, accuracy stalls at some level. That's why we need the next rung.

3Standard

One hidden layer — antennal lobe → mushroom body → output neuron

Model: 2 inputs → H hidden → 1 outputFruit fly: mushroom body circuit

In the fruit fly

Signals from the antennal lobe fan out to thousands of Kenyon cells in the mushroom body. Each Kenyon cell picks up input from a few glomeruli and responds to its own "odor combination." Output neurons gather those responses and decide whether to approach or avoid. Learning happens mainly at the Kenyon cell → output neuron connections.

In a neural network

Insert a "hidden layer" between the input and the output. Each hidden neuron draws one line on the plane, and by combining those lines the output neuron can build boundaries that bend or form bands. This is the multilayer perceptron (MLP).

Memorizing vs. understanding — training data and validation data

A student who saw the exam questions in advance can score 100 on that exam and still prove nothing about their real ability. Models are the same. So we split the data in two. We adjust the weights using only the training data (filled dots), and grade with validation data (hollow dots) that was never used for learning. The validation score is the true measure of skill.

  1. Underfitting: even the training score is low. The model is too simple. The hard problem on rung ② is an example.
  2. Just right: training and validation scores are both high and close to each other.
  3. Overfitting: the training score is high but the validation score is low. With too little data, or wrong labels mixed in, the model memorizes individual examples instead of the rule. Pick "Little, noisy data" to see for yourself.

Common ways to prevent overfitting are adding more data, stopping training when the validation loss starts rising again (early stopping), and not making the model bigger than it needs to be. Fruit flies face a similar problem: so that they don't over-generalize from a single chance experience, long-lasting memories form only after repeated training. In real deep learning, besides validation data, people often set aside separate test data that is used only once, at the very end.

Backpropagation in plain words

It's a calculation that takes the error at the output and divides it backward according to "who's to blame." Hidden neurons that are strongly connected to the output neuron, and that were strongly active at the time, take more of the blame. Once blame is assigned, everything — right down to the input → hidden connections — is corrected at once.

Fruit flies don't do this backward division. The connections into the Kenyon cells are set almost at random and don't change; dopamine adjusts only the last connections (Kenyon cell → output neuron). Choose "Fly-style model" and you'll see it struggles with few hidden neurons, but once you raise the count to 30–60, random lines alone are enough to cut out the band. It hints at why the fruit fly keeps as many as ~2,000 Kenyon cells.

4Multilayer

A visual path with stacked layers — seeing a looming shadow and escaping

Model: layer upon layer, like a CNNFruit fly: compound eye → lamina → medulla → lobula → giant fiber

In the fruit fly

When a fly swatter approaches, a fruit fly takes off in an instant. The ommatidia of the compound eye measure brightness, the lamina splits signals into brightening and darkening, T4 and T5 neurons in the medulla and lobula plate compute the direction of motion, and LPLC2 neurons in the lobula detect "motion spreading outward in all directions" — that is, an approaching object. This signal passes through the giant fiber neuron and becomes a jump.

In a neural network

It's built just like a convolutional neural network (CNN). Each layer repeats the same calculation over a small area at every position (weight sharing), and as you go up the layers the field of view widens and the features become more complex: brightness → change in brightness → direction of motion → looming. Each layer on its own is simple, but stacked together they make a hard judgment.

Compound eye · brightness→ Lamina · brighter/darker→ Medulla · lobula plate · motion direction→ Lobula · looming detection→ Giant fiber · jump
Object to show
View angle
Speed

This is a computational model simplified for explanation. The ommatidia are reduced from about 800 to 169, and the direction of motion is computed directly from the speed of the object's edges. Real T4 and T5 neurons work out direction by comparing signals from neighboring ommatidia with a time delay.

Why stack multiple layers?

It's hard to write a rule that detects an "approaching object" straight from the brightness values of the ommatidia in one step. The object might be dark or bright, on the right or on the left. Instead, we break the problem into easy pieces and give one to each layer.

  1. Layer 1 (lamina): did this ommatidium just get darker, or brighter?
  2. Layer 2 (medulla · lobula plate): compared with neighboring ommatidia, which way is the edge moving?
  3. Layer 3 (lobula): is there outward motion in all four directions — up, down, left and right?
  4. Output (giant fiber): if so, take off now.

With an "object passing by," one edge moves outward while the opposite edge moves inward, so it doesn't satisfy all four directions. That's why the fly doesn't jump even if the object is large and fast. A CNN that recognizes a cat in a photo stacks its layers the same way: lines → edges → parts → whole shape.

Key differences from this page's point of view

The ladder on page ① (ultra-simple → multilayer) was the MLP and CNN side of the story. Alongside it, the fly-style model offers a different answer: "even without training the hidden layer, making it wide and sparse works quite well." In machine learning, random features, extreme learning machines (ELM) and reservoir computing share the same idea. The fruit fly brain also has circuits that handle time, like RNNs and LSTMs (the central complex), but the models on this page make one-shot judgments.

What to take away from this example

Both models got 100% of the training drawings right, but on the validation drawings the fly-style model scored 83% and the MLP 89%. This difference (the gap between training and validation) is the degree of overfitting. Even though the fly-style model has only about 36% as many learned connections as the MLP, its validation accuracy doesn't lag far behind. What it shares with Teachable Machine

The flow is the same: make classes → collect examples → train → judge new inputs.

How it differs

Teachable Machine trains only a small classifier on top of features already learned by a large image model (MobileNet) — this is transfer learning. In this example, two models learn 16×16 drawings from scratch. That makes it well suited to simple shapes, but not to complex inputs like photos.

One-page summary — the four-rung ladder

The higher the rung, the more connections the model has, the more complex the boundaries it can draw, and the deeper the meaning it can extract. The fruit fly's senses → nerves → brain → behavior hold all of these stages in a single body.

Limits of the analogy. Real neurons exchange signals as electrical pulses, and the same connection can behave differently depending on timing and the body's state. Check questions (click to expand the explanations)

Q1. What is the output of a perceptron with two inputs (1, 0), weights (0.8, −0.5) and threshold 0.5?

Sum = 1×0.8 + 0×(−0.5) = 0.8. Since 0.8 ≥ 0.5, the output is 1.

Q2. Why can't a perceptron solve the "band-shaped" problem (food only in the middle)?

A perceptron divides with only a single straight line (plane). A band needs two boundaries, one on each side, so one straight line can't separate it. A hidden layer creates several boundaries and combines them.

Q3. If training accuracy is 96% and validation accuracy is 59%, what happened? Name two remedies.

Overfitting. The model memorized the training data. Remedies: any two of collecting more data, using fewer hidden neurons, and stopping training early.

Q4. In the fly-style model, which connections are learned and which are fixed?

Fixed: projection neurons → Kenyon cells (random sparse connections). Learned: Kenyon cells → output neurons (changed by dopamine).

Q5. In Code 2, the fly-style model (4,004 trained connections) reached accuracy similar to the MLP (16,708 trained connections). What does this tell us?

Even with just a wide, sparse, fixed representation, simple drawing problems can be solved well enough by training the output layer alone. However, since the representation itself can't improve, it has limits on harder problems.
